# Rating Drift Detector: Newbie Workaround and Complete Explanation Guide

This guide is written for someone who is completely new to the project and wants to understand not only how to run it, but also what every file, term, ratio, and output means.

The purpose of this project is to act like a credit surveillance tool: it looks at a company’s financial health over time and detects whether the company is becoming weaker before a rating action happens.

It is not a formal credit rating model. It is a transparent early-warning checklist built from accounting ratios and plain-English flags.

---

## 1. What this project is trying to answer

The project is designed to answer questions such as:

1. Which companies are becoming financially weaker?
2. Which balance-sheet and cash-flow indicators are worsening?
3. How do these companies compare with peer firms in the same sector?
4. Is a company showing signs of stress before a rating review or downgrade?

It is helpful to think of this as a credit screening tool for analysts.

---

## 2. Business context

In credit analysis, lenders and rating agencies monitor companies continuously. A company may look fine in one quarter but show early signs of stress in later quarters.

This project tries to detect those early warning signs using a few standard financial indicators:

- leverage
- debt protection
- liquidity
- working capital pressure
- receivables stress
- cash conversion quality

When several of these indicators move in the wrong direction, the project raises a risk score and flags the company for further review.

---

## 3. What the project does in plain English

The project does the following:

1. Reads company financial data from CSV files.
2. Groups data by company and quarter.
3. Calculates important ratios such as debt-to-EBITDA and current ratio.
4. Compares each company’s current quarter with an earlier quarter.
5. Builds a score based on what worsened.
6. Saves the results in a SQLite database.
7. Creates a processed CSV file.
8. Generates an Excel workbook with dashboard and watchlist sheets.

The final result is a dashboard that shows the companies most likely to deserve analyst attention.

## 5. Core terms explained simply

### 5.1 Company ID
A unique code used to identify a company in the dataset.

Example: `C002`

It is similar to a company code or identifier in a database.

### 5.2 Quarter
The reporting period. In this project, each row belongs to a quarter such as:

- `2025-Q1`
- `2025-Q2`
- `2025-Q3`
- `2025-Q4`

This allows tracking changes over time.

### 5.3 Revenue
Total sales or income generated by the company in the period.

It is the top line of the income statement and is the starting point for analyzing business growth.

### 5.4 EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization.

It is a measure of operational earnings before financing and accounting non-cash charges.

Why it matters:

- used to understand operating performance
- often used in leverage analysis
- helps compare businesses across operating models

### 5.5 Total debt
The amount of borrowing a company owes.

This may include loans, borrowings, and debt instruments.

### 5.6 Interest expense
The cost of borrowing.

This is the amount a company pays as interest on debt.

### 5.7 Current assets
Assets expected to be converted into cash within a year.

Examples include:

- cash
- receivables
- inventory
- short-term investments

### 5.8 Current liabilities
Obligations due within one year.

Examples include:

- short-term debt
- payables
- accrued expenses
- current portion of long-term debt

### 5.9 Current ratio
A liquidity measure:

Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

Interpretation:

- above 1.0 means current assets exceed current liabilities
- below 1.0 means short-term obligations are greater than short-term resources
- lower values suggest tighter liquidity

### 5.10 Receivables
Money owed by customers to the company for goods/services already delivered.

These are a form of credit sales.

### 5.11 Receivable days
An efficiency metric used to estimate how long it takes to collect cash from customers.

Form used in this project:

Receivable Days = (Receivables x 90) / Revenue

This is a simplified approximation. It converts receivables into a roughly 90-day equivalent measure.

### 5.12 Operating cash flow
Cash generated from normal business activities.

This is the cash movement from operations before financing and investing activities.

### 5.13 Cash conversion
A measure of how much of operating profit turns into cash.

Form used here:

Cash Conversion = Operating Cash Flow / EBITDA

A lower value indicates weaker cash generation relative to earnings.

## 6. Core technical accounting definitions used in the model

### 6.1 Leverage
Leverage measures how much debt a company uses compared with earnings capacity.

In the project, leverage is proxied by:

Debt-to-EBITDA = Total Debt / EBITDA

This tells how many years of EBITDA would be required to repay debt.

Interpretation:

- high ratio = more indebtedness relative to operating earnings
- used heavily in credit analysis to judge debt burden

### 6.2 Interest coverage
Interest coverage shows how comfortably a company can pay interest on its debt.

Interest Coverage = EBITDA / Interest Expense

Interpretation:

- higher is better
- lower means the company has more difficulty covering debt costs

### 6.3 Liquidity
Liquidity refers to the company’s ability to meet short-term obligations using short-term resources.

The project uses the current ratio as the liquidity gauge.

### 6.4 Working capital
Working capital is:

Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities

A company with positive working capital generally has enough short-term resources to meet nearby obligations.

### 6.5 Cash conversion quality
This describes how well the company turns operational earnings into actual cash.

A company can post good EBITDA but still struggle if operating cash flow is weak.

### 6.6 Risk score
The project assigns points for signs of financial stress. These are not market credit ratings; they are internal risk signals.

The score is a simple additive model, for example:

- high leverage adds points
- weak interest coverage adds points
- low current ratio adds points
- stretched receivables adds points
- poor cash conversion adds points

The total score is then grouped into risk bands:

- High: score >= 45
- Moderate: score >= 20
- Low: score < 20

### 6.7 Risk band
This is the category assigned to the risk score.

It is a simplified decision label for analyst triage, not a formal rating.

### 6.8 Watchlist
The watchlist is a list of companies flagged for closer attention.

In the project, a company appears in the watchlist if its financial ratios or cash flows deteriorate materially.

### 6.9 Credit surveillance
This is the practice of continuously monitoring borrowers for signs of deterioration in credit health.

The project is a surveillance tool, not a final rating recommendation.

## 7. How the risk logic works

The project computes a company’s latest metrics and compares them with historical values, especially the first quarter in the dataset.

The following rules are used in `src/pipeline.py`:

### 7.1 Leverage trigger
If debt-to-EBITDA > 4, it adds a large number of points.

If debt-to-EBITDA > 3, it adds modest points.

This indicates rising debt burden versus operating earnings.

### 7.2 Debt protection trigger
If interest coverage < 2, it adds heavily.

If interest coverage < 3, it adds moderate points.

This means the company may have trouble covering interest costs.

### 7.3 Liquidity trigger
If current ratio < 1, it adds a significant penalty.

If current ratio < 1.2, it adds some points.

This indicates a weak short-term balance sheet position.

### 7.4 Receivable stress trigger
If receivable days increase materially from the prior period, it adds points.

This often suggests slow collections or weaker working-capital quality.

### 7.5 Cash conversion trigger
If cash conversion < 0.25, it adds points.

This means the company is generating relatively weak cash flow compared with EBITDA.

## 8. How the project calculates the main ratios

All formulas are simple arithmetic ratios.

### 8.1 Ratio helper
The project uses a helper called `ratio(numerator, denominator)`:

```python
def ratio(numerator: float, denominator: float) -> float:
    return round(numerator / denominator, 3) if denominator else 0.0
```

This means:

- if denominator is not zero, divide and round to 3 decimal places
- if denominator is zero, return 0.0

This is a common safe approach when dealing with financial data.

### 8.2 Debt-to-EBITDA
```python
debt_to_ebitda = ratio(debt, ebitda)
```

Example:

- debt = 370
- EBITDA = 42
- 370 / 42 = 8.81

This indicates a high leverage burden.

### 8.3 Interest coverage
```python
interest_coverage = ratio(ebitda, interest)
```

Example:

- EBITDA = 42
- interest expense = 24
- 42 / 24 = 1.75

This is weak because the company barely covers its interest cost.

### 8.4 Current ratio
```python
current_ratio = ratio(current_assets, current_liabilities)
```

Example:

- current assets = 355
- current liabilities = 350
- 355 / 350 = 1.014

This is just above 1.0 and therefore vulnerable.

### 8.5 Receivable days
```python
receivable_days = ratio(receivables * 90, revenue)
```

Example:

- receivables = 198
- revenue = 440
- (198 * 90) / 440 = 40.5

This suggests it takes a long time to collect receivables.

### 8.6 Cash conversion
```python
cash_conversion = ratio(operating_cash_flow, ebitda)
```

Example:

- operating cash flow = 8
- EBITDA = 42
- 8 / 42 = 0.19

This is poor because cash generation is weak relative to earnings.

### 8.7 Revenue growth
```python
revenue_growth = ratio(rev - prior_revenue, prior_revenue)
```

This tells whether revenue is growing or shrinking relative to the previous period.
